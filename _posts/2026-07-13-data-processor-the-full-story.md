I didn’t think I wanted to work in Banking. Sure, I liked numbers, but banking was its own beast.

I started in the back office of the Shared Services Department (SSD) as a Statements Clerk. It was just as it sounds. I printed bank statements for customers, folded them, and sent them in the mail. Six months into my job, just as my probation period was about to end, my manager, the manager of the department, asked me if I wanted to cross-train as a Data Processor. At the time, all I knew was there was a chance to make more money. So, I said sure, thinking “How bad could it be?” The next few years of my life would prove to be some of the most challenging. Not just because of the job, but everything else going on in the world at large.

## The Early Days

I remember the department was separated. Statements was in its own room, away from the rest of the department. I would come to work and be unaware of the work being performed outside of those doors. When I first sat at what would become my desk felt like I was new all over again. I’m not the most talkative at first. I like to get a sense of the room before I do. So, I came, did my tasks, and went home.

My first day, I started out doing salary rejects. We posted salaries via a system called Distribution Details. This requires each customer to have a setup in the system. Their salary would come in and would be read by the system, and based on the instructions set, then that’s where the money would go. You could send your salary with an old account number, and if the setup was correct, then it could still be posted to your new account. For those customers with loans or credit cards, your setup could include instructions to post a portion of your salary to the loan and the rest to your savings or chequing account. For new customers who aren’t set up yet, they would reject. For customers with a closed loan, the payment would be rejected.

There were many reasons a salary could be rejected. It was the job of the Data Processor to research the entry, get the details for it from the session file, and then research it in the system to find the correct customer and where it should be posted.

I remember when I first started, there would be 60-70 rejects per session. There were 7 sessions per day: 8:15, 9:00, 9:30, 11:30, 1:30, 3:30, & 4:45. There were also salary rejections from salaries being posted by the Salary Clerk. We averaged around 200 – 300 rejects a day. Sometimes I’d be in the middle of completing the rejects from the previous session when a new batch would show up on my desk.

There were many ways to post transactions. We would manually post through batches, a program to post large batches called FOXTROT, Distribution Details for salaries and deductions, and SAP – Salary Application Program. Each of these had its own distinct process, and learning them all, rotating between each of them week after week, day to day, was a new challenge.

I remember the end of my first week. I was working 10 - 6, and I saw the other Data Processors weren’t moving. The day had ended, but there was still work to complete. That was my first lesson that the day is complete when the work is complete, not because the clock strikes a magic number. I stayed, understanding that we all leave when the work is done. My leaving on time would mean they stayed longer. My sacrifice benefited the team. I still have the email they sent the next day thanking us for the sacrifice. It was a small one at first. What was one afternoon?

I started logging all my overtime in my desk calendar, so as not to forget it by the next week. I also highlighted our pay days as a motivation. I could look at the calendar and know what I was working towards.

## A Practical Joke

An early experience was when a coworker swapped phones with me. I didn’t know why. He was previously a Data Processor and someone the branches had come to call on. He gave me his number as a practical joke, and it stuck. I remember the first time someone called the line asking for him. I said, "No, this is Alejandro". They were looking for a salary. I haven’t been there for a week yet. I had no idea how to help them. I tried to be as professional as possible and put my hand over the phone and asked out on the floor if someone could help me find whatever it was they were looking for. That started a practice that I appreciated. I could call out on the floor from a phone call, and someone who knew the details about what I was looking for would answer. We all worked on the same system, but different aspects, so I might know something someone else doesn’t. The internal customer on the phone doesn’t know that. All they know is they are looking for that information and expect me to be able to produce it.

This quickly helped me to learn how to navigate the bank’s system. As a Data Processor, you have a vantage point that few in the bank have. Not to be abused, but you can understand a lot about the business if you understand what you’re looking at.

One thing about this job, this position, was that it was very technical. Even now I find it hard to discuss what we did in a way that can be translated to other professionals. If you have never worked in SSD or in digital payments, you just don’t get it.

I had a great team around me; a rag-tag group of misfits is how I saw us. We were the kids no one else wanted, and yet we had a set of skills no one else could perform. We were special. And they welcomed me and helped me however they could. When I would call out on the floor, someone would answer or come to my desk and help me research something. I got a better sense of my desk through those phone calls faster than I would have just performing the tasks.

## The Grind

I learned quickly that if I wanted to leave on time or at a reasonable time, I was going to have to be fast and accurate. The keypad on the right of the keyboard became my best friend. My fingers became nimble, moving faster than I could think about it. Once I saw the number, my fingers went. I still know that part of the keyboard with a blindfold on. I tried getting that good with the letters but never quite like how I could move with the numbers.

## The Cost

Early on, within the first month of cross-training, I realized that my social life was going to be affected. I worked overtime just about every day my first month, even up to 4 ½ hours of overtime in one day. I had put in a 12-hour day, and it wouldn’t be my last. I was thrown into a workforce that was poorly managed, and I couldn’t focus on that just yet because I was just learning how to swim through all of it. I couldn’t make plans because I couldn’t guarantee I would get off in time. I tried at first and eventually learned not to make any plans earlier than 10 o’clock. I couldn’t even get a haircut as my barber went by appointments. I was too scared to schedule something throughout the week because I couldn’t guarantee I would get off in time or even be able to step out for lunch.

## A Distinct Job

A point of annoyance for me was the fact that we were considered the same as the Data Processors in the branch. We did have some overlap, but so much of our work was unique to the work of the department. Our job descriptions mirrored theirs. Without the proper context, you think we are the same, and we were not. We posted so much work that we needed a program called FOXTROT to help post faster. No one else in the bank had it.

We saw the sessions and the transactions coming in. We manually posted entries to customers’ accounts daily. There was a certain risk involved in our work that our pay didn't reflect. We were neglected in ways that showed they didn't understand what we did. We deserved a distinct job title, a proper description, and pay that matched.

## Signing the Contract

For the past six months, I had been the best Data Processor they had seen. I was confident that once this cross-training was completed, the official Data Processor contract would be nice. I was severely disappointed. Sure, it was a 22% increase, but I went from making $19k to $23k. What was the point?

I almost didn’t sign it because I couldn’t believe that after all the work, they had already seen me produce, all the overtime I had put in, they gave me that contract and said: "This is how much we value you". The alternative was to go back to folding statements, and they had already been saying that they weren’t going to be printing statements for much longer. I had a difficult choice, and I was young with no experience dealing with management and understanding my value. Honestly, this department had benefited greatly from that same system, young kids just looking to work and not understanding their value. When you don’t know that you can speak up and change things, you stay and end up in situations you don’t deserve to be in.

I felt like I had proven myself to not be someone who was mediocre, but someone who performed well and deserved to be compensated for good work. The increase was significant, yet it also begged the question of why I was starting so low? Who could live off that? Then the larger question for me came from seeing the range for the position. The potential salary for a Data Processor could be up to $35k, and the low end was $23k. They gave me a contract that said we see you at the very beginning of what a Data Processor is or could be. I would have been happy with something that was in between the end and the middle. It would have shown they respected my promise of what I could bring to the team with continued work and valued my presence. An acknowledgement of the hard work I had already put in.

I struggled for a long time with this contract and the statement I got from it. I could see the corporate side — how do you defend giving someone such a significant increase? If they gave me, say, $26k or $27k, a potential 37% or 43% raise. I felt I deserved it, but what could a manager say to a VP to make that amount make sense? Only in hindsight could someone have seen the appropriateness of it. What I eventually accomplished, I feel, would show why I would have deserved it.

I didn’t have anything to argue with. I had no degree, no skills that could make the work easier, not yet. I was a novice with no experience and no credible standing to say why I deserved more. I feared speaking up about it. The fear of what management would say at such a request. They already seemed pleased with the fact that it was a 22% increase.

To anyone reading this: If you are early in your career, please know you have value. A job at a workplace does not determine your value. Just as they are getting something from you, you are looking for something from them. Know what you want when you go to the interview, and let it be more than just a job because I can assure you some jobs will break you if you don’t know what you signed up for. Ask questions in the interview; know if the place is fit for you. You might even end up like me and realize that it may not be great, but it is experience, and that is fine. If you know what you are doing and have a plan. Don’t get comfortable somewhere that you don’t belong. I signed the contract. What else was I going to do? They had to pay me for the overtime they owed me. I needed the money. It was compensation for everything I had already endured, even if I felt like I deserved more. I didn’t have plans to stay long. I knew I was going to need a change. That life wasn’t sustainable, and I needed to prioritize myself.

## Communication

One aspect of this experience that was particularly frustrating was the lack of action or acknowledgement of the real issues. We worked overtime regularly. Not because we wanted to stretch the bank’s pockets and found some loophole to get paid more. The work just wasn’t being managed properly. We were doing our part, getting the work done. However, what was management doing? Are they not seeing this and thinking something needs to change? Do they not think that we are tired of all this extra time in the office?

The truth later revealed itself when a relatively new team member who came from the branch had a conversation with us. They said that they thought we liked the overtime. That was so infuriating to hear because of how inaccurate it was. If I could guarantee that I would only work the hours I am required by my contract, get the work done, and go home on time, guaranteed, over never knowing when I’m leaving and getting an extra $50 – 100, I would take going home every single time, no questions asked.

Something that was not clear to me at that time was communication and how important it was. I felt it was my management’s responsibility to see the issue and then come to us and have a conversation to address it. I would regularly ask my supervisors what management had said about issues raised, and the response was never what was expected. Their solutions were always shift-based. They felt we were just coming in to work too early and needed to stay later. They tried to make all of us come in from 12-8. We didn’t like that because the days we didn’t need to be there that late wouldn’t help, and what about the work that comes earlier? Now we would just come to work with a stack of work waiting for us. These solutions showed a lack of understanding of the real issues, despite trying to communicate them.

We didn’t have data team meetings. We didn’t have a chance to present our issues to management directly and see their response. There was always a middle person. I feel like that contributed to a disconnect. Without having experienced it, you wouldn’t understand the frustration. Then management never sat on the floor with us. They never spent time seeing what our days looked like. If I said I got 100 FOXTROT journals, that just sounds like a number, irrelevant to anything else. What that means to a Data Processor is I’m about to sit at my desk for the next hour and a half posting, with a chance that some of these journals won’t post due to preparation issues.

They tracked the manual work that was posted, counted it, and that was a measure of how much work you did. The SAP was also counted by entry. The number of Distribution Detail Rejects was counted. All of these were counted- not the foxtrot journals, not even counted, not by entry but by journal. It wasn’t a true measure of what work had been done. You were missing a large portion of the picture. How could you say what I did if you don’t count it/ track it?

## The Report          

Another issue I later realized was what was being communicated to the higher-ups. We prepared a monthly report that spoke about certain statistics. I would prepare a portion of this report as a Data Processor (should have been the Data Supervisor's job) and a separate portion once I cross-trained as an SSD Clerk. What I eventually realized was that a large portion of our work was not being tracked. FOXTROT journals weren’t mentioned, and key elements like session delays, session size, and rejects weren’t mentioned. These matters seriously could make or break our day. The difference between 40 FOXTROT journals for the day and 150 is significant.

Even now, I still can’t track how many journals we posted on any given day. That’s one person’s role on the data team, just to post journals, and no one is tracking their work. It could very easily look like it was based on the reports that were prepared that individuals didn’t do any work that day. How can you speak to our work if you’re not properly tracking it?

Then the issue is that this is the report that upper management reads. They don’t know what we do day-to-day outside of what is presented to them. Reading this report would make it seem like things are running smoothly when they really weren’t. It would make explanations seem simple. If they wonder, well, based on the report, if things are good yet they are working overtime, then what is the issue? Management could blame simple issues, not truly speaking of what was happening. I am certain that even to this day, if I were to speak to them about the issues we faced, they would be shocked to know everything that happened.

## A Young Man, not just a Young Professional

The whole team had gone through a major system upgrade months prior to my joining the team, which resulted in long days, significant overtime, and weekends worked. They were scared and angry. Many had a disposition of neglect from management. They felt management only saw their own agendas and didn’t care for the employees or what they had to say. You could see it in their attitude and their approach to work.

That disposition eventually became my own. After all that I had experienced with management, I was disgruntled as well. We would have conversations on the floor and discuss our frustrations regularly. We worked after normal hours, so management would have already left for the day. We could speak as we pleased.

## The Enemy

What that brought about was a strong feeling of togetherness for the team that was there and strong disdain for management and anyone who didn’t work the way we did. Many were disgruntled with branch members calling. They wouldn’t answer the phones because they felt the branches called too much, that it wasn’t their job to answer their queries. They resented the other team members who had an 8-4 shift consistently. We would hear them leaving for the day, sometimes laughing amongst themselves. We would all be on the other side going “must be nice!” Everyone who didn’t work with us was essentially the enemy.

It was difficult to remain positive with that kind of mentality being so strong amongst the team. I answered phone calls regularly because, despite them not knowing what we go through, they have customers in front of them with these queries, and ultimately that’s who we were working for. I had to fight hard within myself to have a different outlook than everyone else, even so bold as to speak up at times on the floor and to spread a different narrative.

At the time, that was a great fear of mine. I grew up different than most and was often afraid to have the views that I did and would keep them to myself. This felt like an opportunity to stand up, be bold, and unafraid of the reaction. I quickly learned a different lesson. While it was good for my own personal growth to be bold in my stance and different despite the crowd, the crowd wins because of group thought. We as humans can’t fight it, no matter how hard we try. A noble effort, but effort better utilized elsewhere.

So, I would keep my thoughts to myself now, not out of fear but because that wasn’t the time to share them. I would find more strategic ways of introducing new thoughts to the team. Sometimes speaking with one team member at a time, getting them to see my view and share theirs as well. This worked better than expected, and eventually I felt like the team shaped up a bit. In some instances, they weren’t as cynical.

I felt many of my views as a person were challenged through these team discussions, even keeping positive within myself despite all the negativity. It really broke me down and showed me my core, who I really was when tested. Despite the challenges, I was still positive. I still had my principles that I stood by and lived by, and I found ways to continue to be myself despite the dynamics of the floor. I eventually stopped joining in on the discussions as much. I wouldn’t get caught up in the talk. It only riled up my own anger and frustrations and offered no solutions. I would instead think of ways to address these matters.

 I found myself talking to management more. I wouldn’t just be frustrated; I would express my concerns. I would tell them some of the matters we experienced, and if I heard a good suggestion on the floor or had one myself, I would share it with management and see their reaction. Not everything was heard and implemented, but I also wasn’t sitting around just being frustrated with no outlet.

Some solutions didn’t need management’s involvement. Sometimes I would just do things differently myself to avoid the frustration. Other times, I would speak with different team members and get a better understanding of what had happened to see how we could avoid it in the future or better communicate about it.

## The Aspirations of a Young Man

When I joined the team, I aspired to go to Synergy and take their A+ certification course. I wanted to start in IT. With my schedule never consistent, I couldn’t see myself paying for a course I could miss because I was at work and eventually not pass. I had dreams of being able to afford my own travel, taking solo trips in my early 20s. The aspirations of a young man who was also navigating being a young professional.

## The Challenge

I had a unique challenge for myself. Rather than seeing every issue as a new frustration, I saw things differently. When a session would get delayed, and a stack of rejects was coming my way or a stack of manual entries to post, I would challenge myself to see how fast I could get through it all. It would be a personal accomplishment. Those small challenges made the work easier on days that weren’t heavy because I had become so efficient that it was a breeze.

I felt like leaving so often; I almost did as well. As I was gearing up to make the decision, the pandemic hit. With the uncertainty of job security at the time, I decided to stay and tough it out. There was the fear of possibly not working and a fear of marketability. So much of what I had done I had no idea how to explain to someone else who hadn’t worked in it, let alone be able to write it on a resume in a way that made sense. I eventually stopped looking for a way out and found myself finding ways to make the most of a difficult situation.

## The Pandemic Pause

The pandemic changed a lot for the team. We were constantly asking management to hire a fourth Data Processor to alleviate the overtime and heavy workload, and the pandemic saw them try to reduce us to two for a time. We would rotate every 3 days. I worked two days, got the next day off, and the same for the other data processors. We were confused, more disorganized than ever before. Because we weren’t there the day before, we didn’t know what we were walking into. One of the beauties of the team was how we worked together. With some team members not at work, you could feel it in the way the work got done.

Some people handled sessions better than others, and you would see it in the rejects or how long it took them to complete the session. Sometimes you would see rejections from entries that we knew weren’t supposed to be uploaded. Other times you would see entries that were taken out to be posted manually that were meant to be uploaded. This lack of uniformity across all sessions and people who managed sessions made our workload heavier as Data Processors. It was not our place; we rarely said anything, but we felt the frustrations.

We got into a habit of calling out sick. When we would have a heavy day, you can guarantee the next day someone will call out. When that happened, that meant those that did come to work would have a second hard day in a row just because we were short a person. Eventually we started getting petty about the days we took, not because we were tired or close to burnout but because we wanted to get back at the person who called out, for them to experience what they put us through when they weren’t there. That cycle went on for a while, and it was unspoken, but we knew amongst ourselves what we were doing. Eventually I stopped being petty and only took days when I genuinely couldn’t make it.

The pettiness didn’t help and only brought discord amongst the team. There were only three of us. If we didn’t find a way to work together, we would only have another layer of frustrations to deal with. It didn’t make the work any easier, and the work was the problem, not the team members.

## One Person

I developed a mentality of not focusing on my team’s shortcomings. Sure, they were still petty at times amongst each other, but it only took one person to make a change. We started talking to each other more, tackling the work as a team and not in silos. The person on rejects wouldn’t only do rejects. If they finished rejecting and there was other work to do, they would pitch them in until the next set of rejects came. Same for the person on manual and foxtrot. I started it.

It felt like the other two team members were of the mindset: “Well, I can’t guarantee that they would do it for me if I did it for them, so I won’t do it for them”. I took the brunt of it first. They didn’t pitch in right away. Eventually, after seeing my demeanor and not complaining about it and how they both benefited, they would do it for me and eventually would do it for each other.

I would sometimes work overtime just to make sure they didn’t. If I was on the 9-5 shift, I would stay back an hour to help with any work if it looked too heavy to make sure the 10-6 could get off at 6 and 11-7 could get off at 7. Those small sacrifices made us a team. We stopped fighting each other and focused on fighting the work and everyone else.

## The Phone

Through many calls and conversations, I developed relationships with various branch team members. I became the person to call if you needed something from SSD. This called for a poise that wasn’t always easy. I told myself that I would always answer the phone, no matter what. That meant even on my busiest days, in my busiest moments, I would pick up the phone and not carry the energy through the phone. Not always easy, and I didn’t always have the time, but I did my best to research what I could for them, knowing only my team had access to the information. I sometimes asked them to just send an email, and I would research the matter when I got the chance. I would complete the research within the week. I tried to respond quickly and get them out of the way, not letting them pile up.

I developed good relationships with the departments that sent journals for posting. So much so that I would call them about journal issues rather than sending an email or notifying management. Some issues could be resolved with a call and a conversation. These relationships would serve me later in my career.

## SSD Clerk

In August 2020, in the heart of the pandemic, management asked me to cross-train in a new position, the SSD Clerk. I had little understanding of what the desk entailed despite working alongside this person for the past year and some. What made it interesting was how particular this individual was about their work. They had their own frustrations outside of what we were going through in Data. I learned quickly about a new perspective on the department and what was really going on.

I went from the person who received the session and the rejects to the person who prepared them. I would now be the person who posted the rejects after the Data Processors were done prepping them. I handled dividends, sessions, and government deductions.

I reviewed cheques entered by the Clearing clerks for accuracy and logged errors to be corrected. A manual process that was tedious and abhorred by me. The sessions were a highlight. I thoroughly enjoyed learning what the other side of the process looked like. It gave me an understanding that in a few months I was able to address matters that had plagued me since joining the Data team.

I learned about the distribution details system that held all the companies we received salaries for. I saw how a simple amendment in this system could fix many rejects and provide relief to the team. I spoke with my supervisors, and once they agreed, I started implementing changes that saw rejects reduce and become manageable.

I tried to get everyone on the same page because each person approached sessions differently. That sometimes resulted in incorrect uploads and a heap of rejects for the Data Processors. I realized that if we managed the ACH room well, the whole team benefited.

The SSD Clerk was like me and cared deeply about the quality of their work. They had been the only people in charge of this level of work for years, so they didn’t take too kindly to me coming in and making changes right away. I instantly saw easier ways to make certain things work.

## Both Sides

Learning how to process sessions was a very fun and eye-opening experience. Seeing the other side of the process gave a new perspective, one that changed my view of our work forever. I was happy to take on sessions and get more practice with them.

The government deductions, commonly known as RGs, came once a month. It made it hard to learn and fully grasp the process quickly since you only got one chance at it. So that took about 4 months to go smoothly. I learned more about Excel through various processes. Each process taught me more about the department and the work we did and different ways of achieving goals.

I couldn’t fully leave data during this cross-training. I would have to transition throughout the day. I was happy when I did a session because it meant I couldn’t post any of the work from that session, as a segregation of duties.

This cross-training also didn’t come with any stipend or increase in salary, yet I filled in for this desk when the official clerk was out of office. I learned the desk so well that I eventually brought some process improvements to various aspects of the desk. Wrote new process flows showcasing the revisions for others to use.

Even after I had learned the full desk, I continued to cross-train, completing tasks as needed. I would take on a blend of work from both desks, data & SSD clerk. During this time, I made many improvements to the session process and tried to get everyone on board with my fellow data processors in mind. I tried to keep rejects between 20 & 30 per session. More consistent days for the data processors.

During my time cross-training as the SSD Clerk, I learned how to process a salary that we handled differently from all other salaries. It was the SSD Clerk’s responsibility, and the process was unique as well. I had been on the receiving end of the process, the rejects and exceptions as a Data Processor, but now I see how they came about. I learned how to read different reports in the system, seeing how these salaries were posted.

## The Lost Customer

One day, my answering of the phone paid off. I had this payment that I would see as a Data Processor from this same company, and I could not identify the customer. We would reject the payment and post it to a suspense GL until we could find the customer. The payment had been rejected every week for at least two years. Then one day someone called looking for this customer’s payment from this company. After they gave me the details, I found out that it was this customer. They had a different last name in the system, and the name was cut off in the file we received. I was so relieved to be able to help them set that customer up and post all those backdated payments to them and out of the GL.

During this time, I had created a process to handle the influx of ACH transfers from other banks with the help of another coworker. We received recognition from our management team and the bank for making a change to a process through a bank initiative called In Your Line of Sight. It significantly helped with the increased workload. It was heavily Excel-based, and I used some of my new skills gained from the SSD Clerk desk to help create the process.

I eventually spent most days balancing both desks, covering sessions when needed, preparing rejects as a data processor, or posting the rejects as the SSD Clerk. I would process dividends, authorize sessions, and process payments. I even trained a Clearing Clerk on the Data Processor desk as I was the most skilled at the time. I balanced all these duties on any given day.

This cross-training was also longer than others. Most cross-training is expected to take six months. However, I technically cross-trained for twenty months. To make it worse, this cross-training was not official. I didn’t sign a letter, and I later learned that HR was unaware of my work. From a bank perspective, I was just a Data Processor, and I was so much more by the end of this era. I cross-trained unofficially from August 2020 until they wanted me to cross-train as the Salary Clerk because you couldn’t cross-train on more than one desk at a time.

## Salary Clerk

Management had approached me to cross-train on the Salary Clerk desk. I had no real interest in it; I was happy with where I was. I asked if I could have some time to decide, and they said: "Sure, but if you don’t accept, we’ll just find someone else who is willing". Why did they say that? Because now I have to take it! No one else was going to learn something new while I could take on the challenge. Prideful, I know. Plus, I didn’t want anyone messing up the process, creating more work for everyone else.

The Salary Clerk desk was hectic. The processes were no different from the work I had covered on the SSD Clerk desk, just different volumes. While you could receive up to 20 dividends a month as the SSD Clerk, you received a minimum of 20 salaries to process a day.

## The Double Standard

Salaries were complicated. Not because the process was hard, but customers sent files outside of the template. We would have to format the files in a way that they could be processed. This was not to happen and was an unspoken rule. Officially, we were to send the files back to the customers and advise them that they were not formatted correctly and have them correct the file. This was difficult, as customers would then call management, and management would ask us to just post the files. This wasn't difficult because formatting was hard. It was difficult because you had to fix about 90% of the files, and each had different issues. This was a stark contrast to the SSD Clerk desk because when we had issues with dividend files, we would send them back and they had to fix them. We did not tamper with those files. To see just a difference in standard within the same department, right across the room, with the same management team, was a shock to say the least.

You receive a salary file, download it, and advise the customer that it was received. Next, you would have to ensure that it was formatted correctly before attempting to process it. If there was something wrong with the format, the system would let you know, and you would have to start all over again. I had some experience with this with the dividend files, as this would happen sometimes. There were far more issues with the salary files, though. Once we made it past the formatting issues, you processed the file, generating a file to be sent to other banks and sent through the ACH portal. As the salary clerk, you initiate the file, and a supervisor was to do a second approval before the file could be sent. All files had to be sent before the last session to be sent that day. If you missed the cut-off time, the file had to be prepared again the next day to be sent. The internal customers were sent through the distribution details system if they were set up, and the new salaries were posted manually by the Data Processors. Once completed, we would need to send a follow-up email informing the customer that the file had been processed, like the dividends for the SSD Clerk.

I would maintain a log of all salaries received each day and when they were processed. I would specify if there was an external file to send and how it was received. Most were digital files. Others were still received through cheques. For the digital files, we would check that the customer’s accounts had sufficient funds for the file to be posted and would prepare a journal at the end of the day with all the salaries processed.

I found this desk so challenging because you were constantly looking at your email and then in the ACH room processing the files. To make it harder, at the time, the 11:30 session was assigned to the Salary Clerk and was usually the biggest session of the day. You had to cover all these salaries and still do your session promptly because your speed affects other people’s work, and customers receive their money. The constant back and forth was taxing. Some days we processed up to 50 salary files. You had little to no time to breathe.

It was at this time that I found myself accomplishing a random goal. One day we found ourselves short-staffed to the point where only three people who could process sessions were in the office, one of them being me. I decided to attempt to process all the sessions that day, just to see if I could. I came one shy. Someone processed 1:30, but I processed everything else! It was like a bragging rights token. We had seen some wild days in SSD, difficult to handle. To be able to do something like that would’ve been impressive. A little upset I never got to claim that title.

I asked the Salary Clerk at the time how they handled it because I worked in this department with them for years now and had no idea this desk was as bad as it was. Now that we had each other, we started to tackle the desk together. I would help, however I could, cover the session so they could keep working on salaries or downloading the files from the email and adding them to the log for them to process in the ACH room.

I eventually started to balance all three desks, while still being a Data Processor, helping with the SSD Clerk when I could, and cross-training and assisting the Salary Clerk. I was moving around, doing whatever was needed. I was a hybrid worker with no real position. I could be doing several different tasks on any given day. On days I didn’t want to post, I would find my way into the ACH room to process a session and give the work to others. A unique position the other Data Processors couldn’t argue with. I would cover sessions for other supervisors or clerks when they were too busy with other work. I became the go-to for any assistance needed across the department. I became a master at my craft, being the person who understood the system from top to bottom. I understood how payments came into the bank; I understood how they were posted and understood the reports and how to research any payment through these reports. I eventually loved research and finding the odd lost payment.

## The Log

I eventually looked at the previous year’s salary logs and saw major changes in recent years. Salaries used to be as light as 10 salaries a day, with the heavy days maxing out at 30 occasionally. At that time, your light days were between 20 and 30 files a day, and heavy days were 50. This increase in work had basically gone unnoticed because no one looked at the log. No one reviewed the log and counted the number of salaries. There was enough work now for two salary clerks, and management needed to know.

## Almost became a Supervisor

Some weeks later, the Data Supervisor was let go. We went for months without a supervisor, and the position hadn’t been posted. I held the Data Processors down. I felt like the team needed stability, so I stepped up and found myself reaching out to management for answers on day-to-day work matters. There was a gap in his absence, and that gap would have meant things were going to fall apart and chaos would reign if someone didn’t step in. At the time, I didn’t consider the position, but I enjoyed the new responsibilities I had taken on.

Regularly communicating with management, handled sessions, and communicated with everyone else who had sessions, ensuring things were still running smoothly. If a session was coming up, I would check to see who was responsible for it. If they weren’t there, then I would check to see who was willing to take on an additional session. If no one was able to, then I did it. If a session was delayed, I would ask if they had notified management, and most times, they hadn’t. So, I found myself most of the time speaking to management, keeping them up to date about any matters going on with our team. I really felt good adding stability during this time.

## The Interview

When they posted the position, I applied. I was young, sure, but I knew that desk better than anyone. Everything I had done so far had to have been proof. I had about two years of experience as a Data Processor at the time, had cross-trained on two other desks at the same time, and could handle the workload of all those desks on a given day if needed. It was undeniable; how could they not seriously consider me?

I prepared for the second time in my life for an interview. I remember watching these videos on leadership interviews and what kind of questions to expect. I prepped my answers and felt I was ready for whatever they could throw at me. When the interview came up, to my surprise, they asked me the same questions they asked me at my first interview for the Statement clerk position. I couldn’t believe it. It felt like a practical joke. No way that was the real interview. It was me with my manager and assistant manager who worked with me for months. They knew my work, and at no point did it come up. It was like I was a stranger being asked to show my proof. I almost didn’t say anything because I felt I didn’t have to. In my mind, I’m saying, “You know me, you know the work that I do”. Safe to say I didn’t get the position, and there was no real explanation as to why.

I was hurt, not just for not getting the position but also for how the interview was handled. How did they expect to continue to work with me after that? I didn’t know it yet, but that was unacceptable. I didn’t understand how bad it was because I had never experienced anything like it.

I was irritated and, for a while, stopped all the extra assistance I was providing to the team overall. They wanted someone else to lead us, so let them take it on. I was just a Data Processor, so let me be just that. For a while, that’s exactly what I did. I posted my work, answered my calls, and let the extra responsibilities sit with whoever was supposed to carry them. 

## The End and The Beginning

### The Decision

I was still cross-training as a Salary Clerk. So, I started to prepare something for a presentation that the actual salary clerk and I would take to them and see what they would do. Before we could present the information, the salary clerk took a position in the branch. Then came the real decision. Management wanted to give me the position officially after my time cross-training. I had a hard decision to make, as my decision also affected others on the team. The Clearing Clerk I had trained had a chance to become a Data Processor and finally get paid as one after working as one on and off for months at this point. And other team members were going to shift if I took the position.

Despite all the cross-training I had done for the last year and a half at this point and becoming this hybrid worker across the department, management wanted to give me this position as a lateral move with no increase. I sat at my desk. I knew how much more work was going to come my way, and this time I wouldn’t be able to shift out of it if I wanted to; it would be my full-time priority. I knew if I took it, there would be no lunch breaks, and if the files had increased that much already, they were only going to grow even more. The biggest slap was the no increase. I already felt the Data Processor contract was a slap, and now it’s years later after so much experience gained. Management relied on me regularly. They knew the quality of work I produced, the work that I could prepare, the research I could handle that no one else could get results for. This was their time to show their appreciation for all the work I had been doing all this time.

I was even working up to ask them for a salary increase before this offer. I knew I wasn’t going to get that now either, even if I did ask and present all the evidence that had piled up at this point. I made a difficult decision and decided to decline. I knew what that meant for the rest of the team. I felt that I wasn’t just deciding about myself and my work; I was making one for the whole team. It was almost like “take it and help everyone out; you’re good at that.” I knew what I was worth. I was willing to continue to officially be a Data Processor rather than be disgraced and disregarded as a Salary Clerk. Management had to find someone else to cross-train and eventually give the position to.

### The Breaking Point

I was covering work for all these positions, covering gaps for everyone, and eventually it took its toll on me. I had one day where I just couldn’t. I came in to work, and both the SSD Clerk & Salary Clerk were out. It wasn’t the first time it had happened, and before I would cover both desks and still assist with Data Processors when I was done. This day, though, I signed into the emails and saw how much work I had and felt just overwhelmed. I pushed through for a bit, downloaded the files, and started to get to work. I got in the ACH room, and I think there was an error, and that was it. I called my new supervisor over and told them I don’t know what I need, but I can’t do this today. I think I was having a panic attack, but it felt so low — like I felt normal but also on the verge of collapse. She spoke to management and sent me home for the day.

It was needed. Working the way I did, I rarely took time off, no sick days, and the weekends weren’t enough recovery. Especially when I found myself in the office on the weekend covering work that hadn’t been done during the week. I was burnt out badly. I started treating myself differently after that. Every day, I took my lunch hour, no matter what, and if I didn’t, I logged it as overtime like I should have been doing all those years before. I was more rested and felt lighter because of it.

### Just a Data Processor

I was just a Data Processor now, and I started to accept this role. My new supervisor was good. She brought stability to the team that I had never seen before. They didn’t need me to be what I was before. I would help wherever I could and just started to enjoy the slower days. I was reaping the benefits of all my hard work. The sessions were smooth, and the rejects were manageable. We Data Processors were getting off on time, and my days didn’t have the weight they used to.

Eventually, I noticed the team was changing. It wasn’t the same team I had met when I joined. We had a new supervisor; two assistant managers left, and at least five clerks. After the Salary Clerk finally left, I started to wonder if there was something for me elsewhere, not wanting to be the last one to leave the ship. I had done my part; the pandemic seemed to be easing up. Not to say leave the bank, but a new department maybe? Then IT posted a position, entry-level. I was nervous. It was one of only two departments I was interested in. Yet, I knew IT had certain prerequisites that were pretty much nonnegotiable. I had never taken the time to take the A+ certification like I had wanted. So, I didn’t apply. Then shortly after, the SSD Clerk announced that they were leaving. My rock in the department was leaving me! After all that cross-training and the relationship we had built, I didn’t know how to continue in the department without her. Amid all of this, I had started testing whether I could still learn.

The year before, I took the ABA General Accounting course offered by the bank. I needed a development goal, and it seemed easy enough. I was scared I couldn’t actually study again after being out of school for 6-7 years now. Did I still have that drive? That edge that used to make me stand out? This was a test for myself too to see if I could study and work. I had wanted to take on studies for years now, but the work schedule just didn’t allow it. I was starting to consider online studies, something self-paced. This was my first taste to see if I could handle it. To my surprise, I passed with 100%. I think the lecturer liked me! It was the kind of confidence booster I needed.

This year I needed another development goal, so I took the ABA Analyzing Financial Statements course. This was more challenging. I knew little about the work and had to read a chapter of text a week and then complete assignments. With my schedule, my only day available was Sunday. I was reading 30-40 pages of accounting & finance in two hours and then answering questions, replying to scenarios, giving my own comments, and responding to other comments. I almost didn’t make the deadline multiple weeks. I learned a lot and was happy I took the course. I also passed this one, not 100% this time but 90%+. I was ready to study again.

## A New Challenge

The bank was going through another system upgrade. We had a new process added that saw new GLs be added that were our responsibility. Management needed a process to reconcile these new GLs and all the entries passing through them and had asked me to look at it. I was happy to. I made two processes and, for the fourth time in my time as a Data Processor, created a process flow for the new reconciliation process. I came in on weekends afterwards to train the supervisors on the process. I recently visited, and four years later, they still use this same process.

This same upgrade shifted the way the Distribution Details system would process salaries. One of the largest salaries we had was going to be significantly affected, like the whole file rejecting regardless of setups. I reviewed the system and made the adjustments needed, sent the instructions to all the Data Processors in the branches, and gave them a month, the time in between the last salary file and the next one, to update the setups for the customers from this company. The response was amazing. When that salary came in, it had fewer rejects than when it used to come in. The branches had listened and worked with us.

Something that normally would have required going through management, getting their approval, and waiting for them to devise a plan. I avoided all of that and went directly to the source. Management was notified and CC’d on the email, but imagine not taking the initiative. The time it would've taken for them to understand the risk, we could have lost the window entirely. To make it even harder, the risk wasn’t even something proven, not on a scale at least. I knew how to do this because I saw a smaller company being affected this way. It had maybe 5 customers’ salaries, and all had rejected despite being set up. I was on rejects, so I researched why this had happened. When I found out, I knew it would affect all salaries. This was just the first. I prepared this same project a few more times for other companies that would’ve been affected by this upgrade. All my experience was paying off in ways that the team couldn’t even see.

## The Top of the Mountain

It was at this time that I started to feel like I had reached the top of the mountain. It felt like there was no challenge that I couldn’t handle. System upgrades changed the system, and I created new processes to address massive entries on the reconciliation, reducing reconciling entries down to less than 5. Addressing potential rejects before the rejects even happen. Coordinating with all branches to make updates, taking calls, and assisting. That’s when I knew I needed a change. Some people see that and say, now I am comfortable. I see that and say I have little left to learn here.

## The Escape

With the SSD Clerk gone, I was the only backup, and I was next up to take their position. I could see myself taking the position with no increase if they decided to go that route. I liked the SSD Clerk desk. It was slow compared to the other desks I had experienced and smoother. If you were organized, the desk wouldn’t overwhelm you. I was happy to accept when they offered. I held the SSD Clerk position for a few weeks. Within the span of a few weeks, the Finance department posted a position. The other department I would consider leaving for. The position was the same level as my current position, but it was 9-5. I hadn’t worked a normal shift in 3 years. I knew whatever it was, it was going to be easier than anything I'd done in SSD.

Other members of the SSD team were leaving too, opening other positions to apply for. Management didn’t want to see me leave and offer me these other positions. They would have been a grade increase, which meant more money, possibly. They were 9-5s as well, but I knew that if I stayed in SSD, I would continue to be who I was, regardless of my official position. I would be covering all these desks even if that wasn’t my position. The only way to escape was to leave completely. So I took my interviews with Finance, met with the CFO who set me up with what would become my online self-paced studies, and didn’t look back.

## The Other Side

The following months were a transition. My body didn’t know how to work those kinds of hours. I didn’t have enough work to keep me busy all day. I didn’t know what that felt like, not to be busy the entire day. I sometimes would sleep at my desk because the work was done and my body couldn’t stay up with nothing to do. My new management team would be concerned, checking if I was okay. I couldn’t explain that I was bored, regulating my nervous system.

I missed the SSD drama and would find myself spending time off there, sometimes even staying back from 4:30 to 6, not working, just chatting with everyone in SSD. I didn’t know what it meant to be out of the office that early at first. I eventually learned to stay away from SSD, even when I had free time. I was still absorbed in it all. I eventually learned to go and be able to be separate. Eventually the gossip meant nothing to me; they were experiencing things I hadn’t encountered, and it wasn’t my responsibility to fix it or get involved. After two years and eight months, it was over. I had moved on.

So many days I questioned if I would ever see relief, if I would survive, who I would be on the other side. Most days I felt like there was no other side—that I would either not make it through or this would be my life. The team had little hope. The energy was negative. Being in that environment day in, day out, you get caught up in it. It's hard to hold onto hope for something better.

This experience challenged my resolve, challenged what I thought I was capable of as a person. I met my breaking point many times and found the nerve to keep going.

In total, I’ve worked over 500 hours of overtime. That’s an additional sixty-two 8-hour days, not including lunch, over the span of three and a half years. That doesn’t feel like it does justice to what that time meant or felt like. I had posted over 138,000 manual entries and made only 213 errors (0.15%), around 21,000 SAP entries, & possibly over 7,000 FOXTROT journals (had to create a measure myself). I had processed almost 300 dividend files, with an average of 3,800+ entries. In four months of cross-training, I processed over 1,700 salary files. Through it all, countless tasks weren’t tracked or logged, and sessions that weren’t officially assigned to me that I processed or assisted with. The numbers are one way to measure it. The feeling of a day that never seemed to end is another.

## What the Desk Taught Me

The Data Processor desk taught me things that can't be captured in a performance review. As in the Bible, there are examples to follow and examples to avoid. I learned how I would want to approach my team as a leader. From a supervisor and up, I would want to take time out of my week, day, month, and just talk to my team. Hear from them about how they are doing and what the work is like from their perspective. Then not just listen but take what I am hearing and act on it. Think of solutions to their issues that would make their days easier, even if that meant something not in my control, but taking a matter further to my management to get it done. Then not just leave it there and say, “Well, I took it to them, and they didn’t act on it”. I would follow up because it is important to my team and hence it is important to me that matters can be heard and acted on, not just to fall on deaf ears. I would communicate progress, not just leave the team in the dark and provide an update once a matter is complete. I know how frustrating that is on the other end, so I never want to bring that to any team I am on.

Work is always less about the work itself and more about your mental disposition towards it. No matter how hard the task is, if you simply look at it and say this is hard and expect someone else to change it for you, you won’t grow. If you simply complain and don’t act, you won’t grow. You must face the challenge head-on and think with a solutions mindset rather than a defeated one. This is important because if you lose the battle in your mind, you’ve already lost, no matter the task.

You are only one piece to the puzzle. We all must play our part. We complement each other by doing our work well. We work even better when we are proactive and communicate openly and regularly. A well-oiled machine runs smoothly when each part functions as it should. The best teams aren’t just the best individuals placed together. Most times it’s the people who understand who they are working with and understand the goal of the team, not focused on their specific role. They step up when needed, guide when needed, and step back when someone else has a better approach. When a team can function like this, they are unbeatable.
